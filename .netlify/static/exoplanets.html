Discovering Exoplanets

Exoplanets are planets that orbit stars outside our Solar System. The first confirmed exoplanet was discovered in 1992, and since then, thousands have been found. Scientists use various methods to detect them, including the transit method (observing dimming when a planet passes in front of its star), the radial velocity method (detecting star wobble), and direct imaging. The Kepler Space Telescope discovered over 2,600 exoplanets during its mission.

Types of Exoplanets

Exoplanets come in many varieties:

  • Hot Jupiters: Gas giants orbiting very close to their stars
  • Super-Earths: Planets larger than Earth but smaller than Neptune
  • Mini-Neptunes: Smaller versions of Neptune
  • Earth-like Planets: Rocky planets in the habitable zone where liquid water could exist
  • Rogue Planets: Planets not bound to any star, drifting through space

The Habitable Zone

The habitable zone, or 'Goldilocks zone,' is the region around a star where conditions might be right for liquid water to exist on a planet's surface—a key ingredient for life as we know it. Finding Earth-like planets in habitable zones is a major focus of exoplanet research, as these worlds might potentially host life. The James Webb Space Telescope is analyzing exoplanet atmospheres for biosignatures.
