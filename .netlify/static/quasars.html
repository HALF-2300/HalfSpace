What Are Quasars?

Quasars (quasi-stellar radio sources) are the most luminous objects in the universe. They appear as star-like points of light but are actually the extremely bright centers of distant galaxies. Quasars are powered by supermassive black holes at the centers of galaxies, surrounded by accretion disks of hot gas and dust. As matter falls into the black hole, it releases enormous amounts of energy.

Discovery

Quasars were discovered in the 1960s when astronomers noticed objects that looked like stars but emitted radio waves and had unusual spectra. They were initially mysterious because they appeared to be moving away from us at incredible speeds, indicated by their extreme redshift. We now know quasars are very distant, very bright galactic nuclei from the early universe.

Early Universe

Most quasars existed in the early universe, when galaxies were still forming and supermassive black holes were actively feeding. As galaxies matured and black holes consumed available matter, quasars became less common. Today, we see fewer quasars because most supermassive black holes have consumed the nearby matter. Quasars provide a window into the universe's youth.
